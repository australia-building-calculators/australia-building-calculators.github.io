## How to Use the Tile Calculator

### Enter the Dimensions of the Area You Want to Tile

Measure the length and width of each floor or wall area in metres. For walls, measure the width and the height you want to tile. You can add several rectangles, circles or triangles, such as separate floors and splashbacks. The calculator applies one tile width, tile length, grout gap and laying-pattern allowance to every area in that calculation, so group areas that use the same tile and layout. Enter the combined area of doors, windows or other openings in the separate openings field; it is deducted from the combined total, rather than measured automatically.

### Enter the Length and Width of Your Tiles

Tile sizes are quoted in millimetres, such as 300 × 600mm or 600 × 600mm. Enter the actual tile length and width from the product details. Check the packaging, as the nominal size and actual size can differ slightly.

### Include Grout Spacing and Tile Gaps

Grout joints are typically 2 to 5mm wide. Entering the grout width gives you a more accurate count, particularly on large areas or with small tiles where the joints add up. Rectified tiles with tight edges can use joints as narrow as 2mm, while rustic or handmade tiles need wider joints.

### Calculate the Total Number of Tiles Required

As you enter or change valid measurements, the result updates automatically. The tool subtracts the opening area from the combined measured area. It divides the net area by the tile module (tile width plus grout gap, multiplied by tile length plus grout gap), applies the selected wastage allowance and rounds up to whole tiles. Individual area rows are estimates before the overall openings deduction.

### Estimate the Number of Tile Boxes to Purchase

Tiles are sold in boxes, and each box contains a set number of tiles or a set area in square metres. Enter the tiles per box or the box coverage, and the calculator tells you how many boxes to order. Always round up to a whole box.

## How Many Tiles Do You Need for Your Project?

### Calculating Tiles for Floors

Measure the length and width of the room and multiply to get the area. For a 4m × 3m room, the area is 12m². With 600 × 600mm tiles, which cover 0.36m² each, you need 12 ÷ 0.36 = 33.3, which rounds up to 34 tiles before wastage.

### Calculating Tiles for Walls and Splashbacks

For walls, multiply the width by the height of the tiled area. A kitchen splashback that is 3m wide and 0.6m high is 1.8m². With 300 × 100mm subway tiles, which cover 0.03m² each, you need around 60 tiles, plus an extra 10 to 15% for cuts around power points and corners.

### How Many Tiles Do You Need for a Bathroom?

Bathrooms have more cuts, because of the shower, vanity and toilet. Add the floor and each tiled wall as separate areas, then enter the combined area of any openings you want to deduct. The tool applies one wastage setting to the full calculation. The default presets are 10% for straight or brick-bond, 15% for diagonal and 30% for herringbone; these are starting allowances, not installation guarantees.

### Calculating Tiles for Kitchens and Outdoor Areas

Kitchen floors are usually simple rectangles, but you need to account for cabinets. Tile under the area that will be visible, not under fixed cabinets. Outdoor areas such as patios and balconies may need slip resistant tiles, and sometimes extra for slopes and edging.

### Calculating Tiles for Multiple Rooms

Calculate each room separately and add the totals. If all rooms use the same tile, order in one go so that the tiles come from the same batch. This avoids slight colour differences between rooms.

## How to Calculate the Number of Tiles Required

### Tile Calculation Formula Based on Surface Area

The formula is:

**Number of tiles = Total area (m²) ÷ Area of one tile (m²)**

Then add the wastage allowance, and round up to the next whole tile or box.

### Calculating the Area of a Single Tile

Convert the tile dimensions from millimetres to metres, then multiply. For a 600 × 600mm tile, the nominal tile area is 0.6 × 0.6 = 0.36m². For a 300 × 600mm tile, it is 0.3 × 0.6 = 0.18m². The calculator adds the grout gap to both dimensions to create the module used for the tile count. A 600 × 600mm tile with a 3mm joint is therefore treated as a 603 × 603mm module, or about 0.3636m². Nominal tile area is still used when converting a tile count into box coverage.

### Dividing Total Surface Area by Tile Area

Divide the surface area by the area of one tile. The answer is the number of tiles needed to fill the space, before cuts and extras.

### Worked Example of a Tile Quantity Calculation

Say you are tiling a floor that is 4m long and 3m wide with 600 × 600mm tiles, a 3mm grout gap, a straight layout and 4 tiles per box.

- Calculate the floor area: 4 × 3 = **12m²**

- Add the grout gap to each tile dimension: 603 × 603mm = about **0.3636m² per module**

- Divide the area by the module: 12 ÷ 0.3636 = about **33.0 tiles before allowance**; the displayed whole-tile figure rounds up to 34

- Apply the calculator’s 10% straight-layout allowance to the unrounded module count: 33.0 × 1.1 = 36.3, then round up to **37 tiles to buy**

- At 4 tiles per box, 37 ÷ 4 rounds up to **10 boxes**. Confirm the actual number of tiles and square-metre coverage printed on the box.

## How Many Extra Tiles Should You Allow for Wastage?

### Why Do You Need Extra Tiles?

Tiles rarely fit a room exactly. You will cut them at walls, corners and around fixtures, and some will break during cutting or installation. Extra tiles also give you spares for future repairs, which matters because tile ranges are often discontinued.

### Tile Wastage for Straight and Diagonal Patterns

The calculator starts at 10% extra for straight or brick-bond layouts and 15% for diagonal layouts. These are editable starting allowances. The required amount also depends on room shape, tile size, installation layout and whether cut-offs can be reused.

### Extra Tiles for Complex Layouts and Cutting

The calculator’s herringbone preset starts at 30%. This is a conservative starting allowance for that pattern, not a guarantee; adjust it to suit tile size, room layout, installer advice and the amount of cutting. Large format tiles also create more waste, as one mistake costs a lot of tile. Rooms with many corners, niches or curves need extra too.

### Accounting for Future Repairs and Replacement Tiles

Try to keep at least one box, or a few spare tiles, after the job. If a tile cracks years later, having the original batch is the only way to get a perfect colour match. Store spares somewhere dry and safe.

## What Factors Affect Your Tile Requirements?

### Tile Size, Shape and Thickness

Larger tiles cover more area but create more waste in small or awkward rooms. Smaller tiles are more forgiving and suit curves and tight spaces. Thickness does not affect the number of tiles, but it matters for door clearances and transitions with other floors.

### Grout Joint Width and Tile Spacing

Wider joints mean fewer tiles per square metre. On small tiles such as mosaics, the joint makes a noticeable difference. On large tiles, it is minor, but still worth including for accuracy.

### Tile Patterns and Installation Layouts

The layout you choose changes the wastage. A simple grid is the most efficient, while diagonals, herringbone and mixed size patterns use more tiles. Decide on the pattern before you calculate your quantity.

### Room Shape, Corners and Obstacles

Rooms with alcoves, curves, pillars and cutouts need more tiles, because each cut leaves an offcut that often cannot be reused. Measure carefully and add allowance for each obstacle.

### Different Tile Sizes for the Same Surface Area

The same area needs very different numbers of tiles depending on size. Before grout and wastage, a 10m² floor needs about 28 tiles at 600 × 600mm, about 56 tiles at 300 × 600mm and about 111 tiles at 300 × 300mm. The calculator’s grout gap changes the effective module count, so use the result for the actual joint width you plan to install. Remember to compare prices per square metre as well as per tile.

These figures exclude grout joints and wastage. Always confirm with the manufacturer.

### How Many Square Metres Does One Tile Cover?

Multiply the tile length by the width, with both in metres. A 450 × 450mm tile covers 0.45 × 0.45 = 0.2025m². A 600 × 1200mm tile covers 0.72m².

### How Many Tiles Are in a Box?

It varies by size and brand. A box of 600 × 600mm tiles often has 4 tiles (about 1.44m²), while a box of 300 × 300mm tiles may have 11 tiles or more (about 1m²). The box lists both the number of tiles and the coverage, so check the label.

### How to Calculate the Number of Tile Boxes Required

Divide your total area, including wastage, by the coverage of one box. For example, 13.2m² (12m² plus 10%) divided by 1.44m² per box is 9.2, so you need 10 boxes. Always round up.

## Tips for Planning a Tiling Project

### How to Measure a Room Before Buying Tiles

Measure the longest and widest points of the room, and sketch a simple plan. Note doors, alcoves and fixtures. Measure twice, and take dimensions at several points, since walls are rarely perfectly straight.

### Choosing the Right Tile Size for Your Space

Large tiles make a space feel bigger and have fewer grout lines, but they need a flat surface. Small tiles suit shower floors, curves and small bathrooms. For wet areas, check the slip rating of the tile.

### Planning Tile Cuts Around Doors and Fixtures

Start the layout from the centre of the room or the most visible point, so cut tiles fall in less noticeable places. Dry lay a few rows before fixing, to avoid thin slivers at the edges.

### Why You Should Buy Tiles from the Same Batch

Tiles from different production runs can vary in shade and size. Check the batch or lot number on each box, and make sure they match. Buying all the tiles together avoids a mismatch.

Before grout and wastage, 10m² needs about 28 tiles at 600 × 600mm, 56 at 300 × 600mm or 111 at 300 × 300mm. The actual calculator result also depends on the grout gap and selected layout allowance.

Each 600 × 600mm tile covers 0.36m², so you need about 2.78 tiles per square metre. In practice, buy 3 tiles per m² for a rough guide, then add wastage.

### How Much Extra Tile Should I Buy?

The calculator starts at 10% for straight or brick-bond layouts, 15% for diagonal layouts and 30% for herringbone. These values are editable starting allowances; complex rooms may need more after the installer reviews the layout.

### How Many Tiles Are Usually in a Box?

It varies, but 600 × 600mm tiles often come 4 to a box (about 1.44m²). Smaller tiles come in higher numbers per box. Check the box label for the exact count and coverage.

### Should I Include Grout Gaps in My Tile Calculation?

Yes, for accuracy. Grout joints of 2 to 5mm add up over a large area. The effect is small for large tiles but noticeable for small tiles and mosaics.

Yes. Calculate each room separately and add the totals. Order all the tiles at the same time to make sure they come from the same batch.
