## How to Use the Brick Calculator

### Enter the Length and Height of Your Wall

Measure the length and the finished height of the wall in metres. For a wall with more than one section, measure each section separately. If the wall steps up or down, measure each stepped section on its own and add them together.

### Select or Enter Your Brick Dimensions

Choose a standard brick size from the list, or enter your own length, height and width in millimetres. A common Australian clay brick measures 230 × 110 × 76mm. Other sizes, such as extruded bricks and blocks, will give different results, so always check the product details.

### Include Mortar Joint Thickness

Mortar joints are usually 10mm thick. The calculator adds the joint to both the length and the height of each brick, so a 230 × 76mm brick face is treated as 240 × 86mm. Using the right joint thickness gives a much more accurate count.

### Calculate the Total Number of Bricks Required

Press calculate and the tool divides the wall area by the area of one brick face, including the mortar joint. The result is the number of bricks needed to build the wall, before any wastage.

### Allow for Brick Wastage and Breakages

Bricks get cut, chipped and broken on every job. The calculator lets you add a wastage percentage, normally 5 to 10%. Use the higher figure for complex walls with lots of corners, piers and openings.

## How Many Bricks Do You Need for Your Project?

### Calculating Bricks for a Standard Wall

A standard single skin wall that is 10m long and 2m high has an area of 20m². At about 48.5 bricks per square metre, you need around 970 bricks. Adding 10% for wastage gives about 1,070 bricks.

### How Many Bricks Do You Need for a Boundary Wall?

Boundary walls are usually taller and longer, and often include piers for strength. Calculate the main wall area and add extra bricks for the piers. A 20m boundary wall at 1.8m high is 36m², or around 1,750 bricks before wastage. Check council rules for height limits and approvals.

### Calculating Bricks for Garden and Retaining Walls

Low garden walls are simple to calculate, using the same wall area method. Retaining walls are different, because they hold back soil and often need engineered design, drainage and approval. Confirm the requirements with your local council or an engineer before you build anything that retains soil.

### Brick Requirements for Different Wall Heights

Course height, including the mortar joint, is 86mm for a standard brick. That is about 11.6 courses per metre. A wall of 1.2m needs roughly 14 courses, a 1.8m wall needs about 21 courses, and a 2.4m wall needs about 28 courses.

### Calculating Bricks for Multiple Walls

Calculate each wall separately and add the totals. This is the best way to handle houses, extensions and garden structures with more than one wall. Subtract openings from each wall before adding.

## How to Calculate the Number of Bricks Required

### Brick Calculation Formula Based on Wall Area

The formula is:

**Number of bricks = Wall area (m²) ÷ Face area of one brick including mortar (m²)**

Then add your wastage allowance and round up.

### Calculating the Face Area of a Brick

The face is the length multiplied by the height of the brick. For a standard 230 × 76mm brick with a 10mm joint, the face including the mortar is 240 × 86mm, or 0.24 × 0.086 = 0.02064m². That gives about 48.5 bricks per square metre.

### How Mortar Joints Affect Brick Quantity

Thicker joints mean fewer bricks per square metre. A 10mm joint is standard. A 12mm joint reduces the count to roughly 46 bricks per m², while an 8mm joint increases it to about 50.5. Small differences add up on large walls, so use the correct joint size.

### Worked Example of a Brick Quantity Calculation

Say you are building a wall that is 12m long and 1.8m high, using standard bricks with 10mm joints.

- Calculate the wall area: 12 × 1.8 = **21.6m²**

- Calculate the face area of one brick with mortar: 0.24 × 0.086 = **0.02064m²**

- Divide: 21.6 ÷ 0.02064 = 1,046.5, or about **1,047 bricks**

- Add 10% for wastage: 1,047 × 1.1 = **1,152 bricks**

You would order around 1,150 bricks, or 1,200 if you want a round figure.

## Understanding Brick Sizes and Types

### Common Brick Sizes and Dimensions

| Brick type | Size (L × W × H) | Approx. bricks per m² (10mm joint, single skin) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard modular clay brick | 230 × 110 × 76mm | 48.5 |
| Extruded or larger brick | 290 × 110 × 90mm | 33.3 |
| Concrete block (half height) | 390 × 190 × 190mm | 12.5 |

These are approximations. Brick sizes vary by manufacturer, so always confirm with your supplier.

### Standard Bricks vs Special-Shaped Bricks

Standard bricks are rectangular and used for most walls. Special shapes include bullnose, cant, arch and plinth bricks, used at corners, window sills and decorative features. These are counted separately, and they are usually ordered in smaller numbers.

### Single-Skin vs Double-Skin Brick Walls

A single skin wall is one brick thick (110mm), and a double skin has two layers, either as two separate skins with a cavity or as a solid wall. A double skin wall needs about twice as many bricks per square metre, so about 97 bricks per m² instead of 48.5.

### How Brick Dimensions Affect Material Requirements

Larger bricks cover more area, so fewer are needed, and the job goes faster. They also need more mortar per brick, but usually less mortar per square metre. Choose a size that suits your design and the way the wall will be built.

## How Many Extra Bricks Should You Order?

### Why Brick Wastage Occurs During Construction

Bricks are cut to fit corners, openings and the top of the wall. Some break during delivery and handling, and some come with defects. Even skilled bricklayers lose a few percent.

### Allowing for Cutting, Breakages and Damage

For simple, straight walls, 5% extra is usually enough. For walls with many corners, piers and openings, allow 10%. A tight allowance saves money, but running short means a second delivery and possible colour variation.

### How Wall Patterns and Bonding Affect Brick Quantities

Stretcher bond, the most common pattern, is the most efficient. English and Flemish bonds use more bricks because they mix header and stretcher faces. Decorative patterns and soldier courses also use more bricks and create more cuts.

### Planning for Future Repairs and Replacement Bricks

Keep a few spare bricks from the same batch for future repairs. Brick colours vary between batches, and some ranges are discontinued. Store the spares somewhere dry.

## What Factors Affect Brick Requirements?

### Wall Length, Height and Thickness

The wall area sets the base quantity. A thicker wall, such as a double skin, doubles the number of bricks per square metre. Always confirm the thickness in the plans.

### Mortar Joint Size and Brick Dimensions

The joint size and the brick size together set the number of bricks per square metre. Check both before you calculate, and do not assume.

### Doors, Windows and Other Wall Openings

Subtract the area of large openings such as doors and windows. A standard door is about 2.0m × 0.8m (1.6m²), and a window might be 1.2m × 1.2m (1.44m²). Small openings can be ignored, because the cuts around them use extra bricks anyway.

### Brick Bond and Construction Method

The bond pattern affects both the number of bricks and the amount of cutting. Cavity walls need two skins of brick, or one skin of brick with a different inner wall. The construction method is usually set by the building plans.

### Wall Design and Structural Requirements

Taller walls, piers, retaining walls and load bearing walls may need engineering, reinforcement and approvals. Always check the building code and local council requirements before you order materials.

## Understanding Mortar Requirements for Brickwork

### How Much Mortar Is Needed for Bricklaying?

As a rough guide, a single skin wall of standard bricks needs about 0.3 to 0.4m³ of wet mortar per 1,000 bricks, including a margin for waste. This varies with joint thickness, brick type and how carefully the bricks are laid.

### What Is a Typical Mortar Joint Thickness?

The standard mortar joint in Australian brickwork is 10mm, both for the bed joints (horizontal) and the perpends (vertical). Keep joints consistent, as variation affects both the look and the strength of the wall.

### Cement, Sand and Water Requirements for Mortar

Common mortar mixes use cement, sand and sometimes lime or a plasticiser. A typical ratio is 1 part cement to 5 or 6 parts sand, with enough water to give a workable mix. The right mix depends on the brick type and the exposure, so follow the supplier’s advice or the engineer’s specification.

### Estimating Mortar Separately from Brick Quantities

Calculate the bricks first, then work out the mortar from the number of bricks. For 1,000 bricks, plan on 0.3 to 0.4m³ of mortar. Order the sand, cement and any additives based on your mix ratio, and add a little extra for waste.

## Frequently Asked Questions About the Brick Calculator

### How Many Bricks Do I Need per Square Metre?

For a single skin wall of standard 230 × 110 × 76mm bricks with 10mm joints, you need about 48.5 bricks per square metre. A double skin wall needs about 97.

### How Many Bricks Are Needed for a 10m Long Wall?

It depends on the height. A 10m wall at 1.8m high is 18m², or about 873 bricks. At 2.4m high it is 24m², or about 1,164 bricks. Add 5 to 10% for wastage.

### How Much Mortar Is Needed per 1,000 Bricks?

About 0.3 to 0.4m³ of wet mortar for 1,000 standard bricks, with a 10mm joint. Allow a little extra for waste and uneven brickwork.

### How Many Extra Bricks Should I Buy?

Allow 5% for simple walls and 10% for walls with many corners, piers or openings. It is a good idea to keep a few extra from the same batch for repairs.

### Do Mortar Joints Affect the Number of Bricks Required?

Yes. Thicker joints reduce the number of bricks per square metre, and thinner joints increase it. A 10mm joint gives about 48.5 bricks per m², while a 12mm joint gives about 46.

### Can I Calculate Bricks for Multiple Walls?

Yes. Calculate each wall separately, subtract the openings, and add the totals together. Then add your wastage allowance to the combined figure.
